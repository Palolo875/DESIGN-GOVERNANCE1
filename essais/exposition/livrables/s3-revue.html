A border is a line that somebody has to be able to read. This issue follows the people who read it, ignore it, translate it, or live in a house it runs through.

We asked for writing from the threshold, whatever its size: a frontier post, a mother tongue, a school gate, the edge of a genre. The pieces we kept disagree about whether a line protects or divides. Most of them cross it anyway, and tell you what it cost and what the weather was like on the other side.

There is no photograph on the cover this time. The cover is set in type, and the type is cut by a line.

Contributors

Eleven writers and translators, in alphabetical order. (All names and biographies are examples.)

Rosalind Achterberg translates from Korean and Dutch and lives in a town with two railway stations. Translator, The Postman of Km 14, p. 68

Callum Aird writes poems and repairs boats on an estuary where the county line runs down the middle of the channel. Weather on the Other Side, p. 92

Selin Aksoy-Berg writes fiction. She grew up between İzmir and Malmö and has missed more connections than she will admit. Transit Hall B, p. 53

Yusuf al-Rahmani translates from Arabic and teaches interpreting. One Word That Will Not Cross, p. 101

Jun-seo Baek is a novelist and a former rural postal worker. This is his first appearance in English. The Postman of Km 14, p. 68

Mirela Dragović writes essays about money, migration and household objects. Small Change, p. 81

Tomás Echeverri is a linguist who has spent twenty years collecting words that exist on only one side of a river. Where the Language Stops, p. 34

Adaeze Mbeki-Lund writes poems and commutes by ferry. Four Ferries, p. 47

Marta Quinteira writes poems in Portuguese and in Dutch. Declaration of Goods, p. 11

Dev Anand Rowe translates from Portuguese and Spanish, and sometimes into them. Translator, Declaration of Goods, p. 11; One Word That Will Not Cross, p. 101

Ilse Varga-Holm writes fiction and works as a land surveyor. The Surveyor’s Daughter, p. 19
